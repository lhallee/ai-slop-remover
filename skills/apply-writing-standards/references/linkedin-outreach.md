# LinkedIn, social posts, and outreach

Use this profile for feed posts, launch notes, project updates, direct messages, emails, and short public statements. For a LinkedIn article or newsletter, use `blogs.md` as the primary profile and preserve its longer narrative form.

## Public post

Read `../../writing-standards/references/storytelling.md`. Decide what this audience will care about before choosing the opening. Use the relevant pillars to find the angle, then build a compact story around the hook, problem, why, action, outcome, and goal. These are ingredients, not six paragraphs or visible labels.

Make the opening lines carry a recognizable problem, specific tension, or consequential result. Put the stakes close enough that an unfamiliar metric or protein name does not have to earn attention on its own. "Our paper is out" supplies timing; the reader still needs a reason to care about its contents.

Keep one main thread. Show how the work addressed the problem and what changed. Explain the connection between a prediction and the actual follow-up result rather than placing them in adjacent paragraphs and expecting the reader to infer it. Use plain language and enough evidence to support the takeaway; link to the paper or detailed account for secondary methods.

State strong results firmly. A caveat belongs where it prevents a material misunderstanding, not in a compulsory final disclaimer. Preserve publication status and distinguish measured outcomes from goals. Credit collaborators specifically, after the story has established their contribution or where their role matters.

End with an earned implication, concrete next goal, or useful invitation and link. Do not require a question, call to action, or generic statement about the future of a field. Avoid engagement bait, empty excitement, exaggerated adversity, unsupported superiority, and a line break after every sentence.

## Launch note

Open with the bottleneck or new capability that matters to the audience. Connect it to what is available now and one or two meaningful proof points. Explain what someone can do with it, then offer a specific route to read, test, try, or collaborate when useful. Features without a consequence do not explain the value.

## Direct outreach or email

1. Open with the specific post, paper, launch, or shared problem that prompted the message.
2. Introduce the sender in one compact paragraph.
3. Explain the technical or professional overlap.
4. Give one distinguishing result with enough context to make it credible.
5. State the motive honestly.
6. Make a low-pressure request to talk.

Warmth should come from specific attention, not praise-heavy language. Default to the shortest version that preserves the trigger, overlap, evidence, and ask. Remove literature reviews, career histories, and extra metrics unless the recipient needs them.

Use conversational fragments and contractions when appropriate. Avoid influencer cadence and attempts to manufacture a personal brand.
